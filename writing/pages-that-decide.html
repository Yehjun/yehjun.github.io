Trend note

Jev, and what cheap judgment could mean for marketing

A new kind of AI model returns a judgment instead of writing text, at a fraction of a cent per call. This note explains what it is and what the first independent test found, then lists a few research ideas it opens up for marketing. If one of them interests you, I'd be glad to work on it together.

Yeh Jun Lee · September 2026 · 8 min read

What Jev is

On September 15, 2026, TypeSafe AI released Jev, which it calls a "System One" model, a nod to Kahneman's fast, intuitive mode of thinking. It is available in early access through a closed API.

The difference from a chatbot is in the output. A language model writes prose for a person to read. Jev writes nothing. You define a question and its possible answers in advance, and Jev returns one of those answers along with a probability for each. Software can use the output directly because it can only be one of the options you allowed.

TypeSafe says the model is trained so that its probabilities are calibrated, meaning that when it says 0.8 it is right about 80% of the time. The company also claims more than 100 times lower cost and roughly 200 times faster responses than frontier language models on classification tasks, in the tens to hundreds of milliseconds. These are the company's own figures.

What an independent test found

A week after the release, researchers at Carnegie Mellon posted an evaluation of Jev used as a judge (Li, Miao, Krishnan, & Padman, 2026). They report that Jev is a ModernBERT-large encoder with a small decision head, a far smaller model than a frontier LLM, and they compared it with GPT-6 on the same judging tasks.

The results were not uniformly good. On JudgeBench, a harder benchmark, Jev trailed by 14.6 points, and the gap grew to 9–20 points on judgments that require checking a derivation or resisting an elaborate but wrong answer. The authors' solution is a cascade:

Why marketers might care

The main change is cost and speed. Here is what the reported figures would mean for an application that makes a judgment every few seconds while someone browses:

My arithmetic from the per-thousand costs and latencies in Li et al. (2026). Thirty judgments per visit is an assumption for illustration. A cascade would cost more than the Jev column, because unsure calls go to the big model — in the paper's preference test the cascade cost about 57% of GPT-6's fee. On a web page, though, there is a cheaper answer to "unsure": do nothing and leave the page as it is.

At the first column's cost, a judgment can be made many times per visitor instead of once. Many marketing decisions have been made once per page load or once per campaign because judgment was expensive. That constraint may loosen.

Ideas worth exploring

None of these has been built or tested. They are starting points, and each would need careful validation before anyone relied on it.

1 · Pages that adapt while you read

A product page could choose among pre-written sections (a spec table, a cheaper comparison, a size guide) based on how a visitor is reading, and act only when the model is confident. The marketing questions are whether this helps people decide, and when responsiveness starts to feel like being watched.

2 · Calibrated judgments as a research tool

Consumer researchers spend a great deal of effort coding open-ended responses, reviews, and ads by hand. A model that returns a label and an honest probability could code at scale while flagging the cases a human should check. The key test is whether its probabilities match human coders' agreement.

3 · Checking every creative variant

Brands now generate dozens of image and copy variants per campaign. A cheap judgment could screen each one against the meaning the brand intends ("does this feel serene?") before anything goes out. This connects directly to my own work on measuring the fit between meanings and sensory cues.

4 · How fast machine decisions feel to consumers

When a service decision (a refund, an approval, a routing) is made instantly by a machine, do consumers see it as more efficient or less fair? A "System One" decision is fast by design, and people may read speed as a lack of consideration. That is an open consumer-psychology question in its own right.

Caveats

  • The cost and accuracy figures come from a vendor and from one preprint that has not yet been peer reviewed.
  • Jev is weaker on judgments that require careful reasoning, so the tasks it suits are narrower than a general-purpose model's.
  • Any use that reads visitor behavior raises privacy and consent questions that need to be designed in from the start.
