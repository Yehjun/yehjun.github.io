Idea & proposed research

Pages that decide while you look

Web pages already react while you read, but mostly through simple rules. Fast, cheap judgment models may make it possible for a page to make an actual judgment about what you need, while you are still reading. This is an idea and a plan for studying it — nothing here has been built or tested yet.

Yeh Jun Lee · September 2026 · 9 min read

Pages react, but they rarely judge

Pages already change while you look at them. An exit-intent pop-up appears when your cursor heads for the close button. A coupon slides in after you scroll halfway. A chat window offers help after thirty seconds. Feeds reorder themselves as you scroll. But these are mostly rules and triggers: if this happens, then show that.

The richer kind of decision — reading what this particular visitor seems to be after and choosing what to show them — usually happens at fixed moments: when the page loads, or after a click, a "load more," or a new search.

Part of the reason is cost. A large language model can make a nuanced call about what a visitor is looking for, but running one dozens of times per visit, for every visitor, has been hard to justify on an ordinary product page — both in money and in waiting time.

What changed

In September 2026 a company called TypeSafe AI released Jev, which it describes as a "System One" decision model. The distinction is useful. A language model writes prose that a person reads. A decision model takes messy input and returns a typed judgment that software consumes: one answer from a pre-approved set of options, plus a probability for its choice.

The numbers the company reports are what make this interesting for marketing. Jev runs on a comparatively small backbone, costs on the order of four cents per thousand judgments, and lands within about three percentage points of a frontier-model judge on preference and factuality tasks — at well under one percent of the cost. Used in a cascade, where confident calls are accepted and uncertain ones escalated to a larger model, it reportedly retains nearly all of the larger model's accuracy.

What caught my attention wasn't the chatbot angle. It was the price. A judgment for a fraction of a cent is a different kind of thing from a judgment for a few cents — cheap enough to spend on a single visitor, over and over, while they read.

Whether those figures hold up independently remains to be seen. If they do, the constraint loosens: when a judgment costs a fraction of a cent, a page could afford to ask "what should this visitor see next?" continuously rather than once.

The page load may never have been the best moment to decide. It was the moment we could afford.

What a deciding page could look like

The version I have in mind is modest:

  1. The page is built from a fixed set of modules — a specification table, a lifestyle image, a comparison with a cheaper model, a review summary, a size guide, a sustainability note. These are the pre-approved options. Nothing is generated on the fly.
  2. The visitor's behavior is the input — where they pause, what they scroll past, what they hover over and abandon, what they searched for to get here. Because models like Jev read text, this behavior would first have to be summarized into a short description the model can read. How to do that well is itself an open design question.
  3. A fast judgment picks the next move: which module to surface, what to emphasize, what to fold away. The decision is a label with a probability, and it can be logged.
  4. When the model is unsure, it doesn't act. Low-confidence judgments either leave the page alone or escalate to a slower, more careful call.

Picture a product page for a winter jacket. You linger on the warmth rating. You scroll past the colors without stopping. You hover over the price, move away, come back. By the time you reach the bottom, the page has quietly brought up a comparison with a lighter, cheaper jacket and folded the size chart away — because that is what someone reading the way you read usually needs next.

The hope is that it would feel less like a page rearranging itself and more like a good salesperson who notices you keep glancing at the price tag and quietly mentions the payment plan — without making you ask.

Why this is a marketing question, not just an engineering one

Even if the engineering works, whether it is a good idea depends on people, and that is where I think the interesting research is.

A page that changes while you look can feel helpful or uncanny. People tolerate being shown a personalized page. They may react very differently to watching one adapt in real time. There may be a threshold where responsiveness turns into a sense of being watched — and where it lies is an empirical question.

Consistency is part of trust. Two colleagues comparing a product would see different pages. For some categories that is harmless. For anything involving price or eligibility it is not, and the modules a model is allowed to choose among need to be designed with that in mind.

Someone has to check whether the judgments are right. A decision model's great virtue is that it commits: it returns a choice and a probability. That makes it straightforward to test. If the model says "this visitor wants a cheaper alternative, 0.8," we can check how often visitors at 0.8 actually do. This is the part closest to my own work, which is about testing whether a model-derived measure tracks what people actually think.

Proposed studies

None of these has been run yet. The plan is to go in three steps, from controlled to real.

Study 1 — Does in-view adaptation help, and at what cost?

An online experiment would show a realistic product page in three versions: static; personalized once at load; and adapting while the participant reads. Crossed with whether the adaptation is disclosed ("This page adjusts to what you look at"). Outcomes: choice, time to decision, satisfaction with the choice, perceived autonomy, and trust in the retailer. The key question is whether in-view adaptation improves decisions without eroding the sense that the choice was the shopper's own — and whether disclosure fixes or worsens that trade-off.

Study 2 — Are the judgments right?

A validation study. As participants browse, the model would make its calls about what each person wants next. Afterward, participants would report what they were actually looking for, and their choices would be recorded. The analysis would ask two things: how often the model's judgments match people's own accounts and their behavior, and whether its probabilities are calibrated — whether its 0.8s are right about eighty percent of the time. A model that is accurate but overconfident would be exactly the wrong thing to put in charge of a page.

Study 3 — Does it work in the wild?

If the first two studies are promising, a field test on sites I have built, such as the SimSang Brand Fit Index. Visitors would be randomly assigned to adaptive or static versions and compared on engagement and task completion. Because I run these sites, a clean randomized test would be feasible.

The bigger bet

In broad strokes, marketing technology has been shaped by what judgment costs. When judgment was expensive, we segmented: decide once for a whole group. When it got cheaper, we personalized: decide once per person. If it becomes nearly free, the natural unit is neither the group nor the person but the moment — what this person needs right now, while they are still deciding.

If that shift happens, I would like there to be evidence about when it helps people and when it quietly takes something from them, before the default gets set.
