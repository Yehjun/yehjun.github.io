Idea & proposed research

Pages that decide while you look

Web pages already react while you read, but mostly through simple rules. A new kind of model — fast, cheap, and built to make judgments rather than write text — may let a page make an actual judgment about what you need while you are still reading. This is an idea and a plan for studying it. Nothing here has been built or tested yet.

Yeh Jun Lee · September 2026 · 14 min read

Pages react, but they rarely judge

Pages already change while you look at them. An exit-intent pop-up appears when your cursor heads for the close button. A coupon slides in after you scroll halfway. A chat window offers help after thirty seconds. Feeds reorder themselves as you scroll. But these are mostly rules and triggers: if this happens, then show that.

The richer kind of decision — reading what this particular visitor seems to be after and choosing what to show them — usually happens at fixed moments: when the page loads, or after a click, a "load more," or a new search.

Part of the reason is cost. A large language model can make a nuanced call about what a visitor is looking for, but running one dozens of times per visit, for every visitor, has been hard to justify on an ordinary product page — in money, and in waiting time.

What Jev is

On September 15, 2026, a company called TypeSafe AI released Jev, which it calls a "System One" model — a nod to Kahneman's fast, intuitive mode of thinking. It is in early access through a closed API.

The key difference from a chatbot is what comes out. A language model writes prose that a person reads. Jev writes nothing. You define a question and its possible answers in advance; Jev returns one of those answers along with a probability for each. Software can use that output directly, because it can only ever be one of the options you allowed.

TypeSafe says it trains the model so that its probabilities are calibrated — so that when it says 0.8, it is right about 80% of the time — and claims more than 100 times lower cost and roughly 200 times faster responses than frontier language models on classification tasks, in the tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Those are the company's own figures.

An independent test

A week later, researchers at Carnegie Mellon posted an evaluation of Jev used as a judge (Li, Miao, Krishnan, & Padman, 2026). Under the hood, they report, Jev is a ModernBERT-large encoder with a small decision head — a far smaller model than a frontier LLM. They compared it with GPT-6 used as a judge on the same tasks.

The picture is not uniformly rosy, and that matters for what follows. On JudgeBench, a harder benchmark, Jev trailed by 14.6 points, and the gap grew to 9–20 points on judgments that require checking a derivation or resisting an elaborate but wrong answer. A cascade would cost more than the Jev column, because unsure calls go to the big model — in the paper's preference test the cascade cost about 57% of GPT-6's fee. On a web page, though, there is a cheaper answer to "unsure": do nothing and leave the page as it is.

The first column is a rounding error on most marketing budgets. The second is not. If the reported numbers hold up outside the benchmark, the constraint loosens: a page could afford to ask "what should this visitor see next?" continuously rather than once.

The page load may never have been the best moment to decide. It was the moment we could afford.

What a deciding page could look like

The version I have in mind is deliberately modest. Nothing is generated. The model only chooses among things a person has already written and approved.

The question the page asks could be as plain as this:

// Illustrative — not Jev's actual API syntax
question: "Given how this visitor has read the page so far,
           which section would help them most next?"
context:  "Arrived from search 'warmest jacket under $200'. Read the
           warmth rating for 14 s. Scrolled past colors. Hovered on
           the price twice, then scrolled back up to warmth."
options:  [warmth_details, cheaper_alternative, size_guide,
           review_summary, do_nothing]

The context line is the hard, unsolved part: turning raw clicks and pauses into a short description a model can read well is itself a design problem worth studying.

Six design rules I'd start from

If this is worth doing at all, it is worth doing in a way people would accept if they could see how it works. These are the rules I would build under, and each one is also a testable claim:

Choose, never writeOnly pre-approved modules. No generated claims, so nothing the page says was invented on the fly.
Unsure means staticThe default is the ordinary page. The model acts only above a confidence threshold, and the threshold is set from calibration data.
Price is off-limitsPrice, discounts, and eligibility never vary by judgment. Two colleagues comparing the jacket should see the same price.
Change out of viewModules change where the visitor isn't looking. A page that moves under your eyes feels like being watched.
Say that it adaptsA visible note and a switch back to the standard page. Whether disclosure helps or hurts is itself a Stage 2 question.
Log every decisionEach judgment and its probability is recorded, so the system can be audited — and so its accuracy can be checked.

Why this is a marketing question, not just an engineering one

Even if the engineering works, whether it is a good idea depends on people, and that is where I think the interesting research is.

A page that changes while you look can feel helpful or uncanny. People tolerate being shown a personalized page. They may react very differently to watching one adapt in real time. There may be a threshold where responsiveness turns into a sense of being watched — and where it lies is an empirical question.

Consistency is part of trust. Two colleagues comparing a product would see different pages. For some categories that is harmless. For anything involving price or eligibility it is not, which is why those are outside what the model may choose.

Someone has to check whether the judgments are right. A decision model's great virtue is that it commits: it returns a choice and a probability. That makes it straightforward to test. If the model says "this visitor wants a cheaper alternative, 0.8," we can check how often visitors at 0.8 actually do. This is the part closest to my own work, which is about testing whether a model-derived measure tracks what people actually think.

The plan

None of this has been run. The order matters: there is no point asking whether an adaptive page feels creepy if the model can't tell what people want. So validation comes first, then experience, then the field — and each stage has a condition under which I would stop or change course.

Why assigned goals make Stage 1 work

The hardest part of validating a mind-reading page is knowing what was actually in the visitor's mind. Asking afterward is noisy and colored by what the page showed. Assigning the goal before people shop sidesteps that: we know what each person was trying to do, so we can score every judgment the model made along the way. The main output would be a calibration plot — for all the times the model said 0.8, how often was it right? A model that is accurate but overconfident would be exactly the wrong thing to put in charge of a page.

The bigger bet

In broad strokes, marketing technology has been shaped by what judgment costs.

When judgment was expensive, we segmented: decide once for a whole group. When it got cheaper, we personalized: decide once per person. If it becomes nearly free, the natural unit is neither the group nor the person but the moment — what this person needs right now, while they are still deciding.

That shift could go well or badly. A page that notices you care about warmth and shows you a lighter, cheaper jacket is doing you a favor. A page that notices you hesitating and turns up the pressure is not, and the technology is the same. If the shift happens, I would like there to be evidence about when it helps people and when it quietly takes something from them, before the default gets set.
