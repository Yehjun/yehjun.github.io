Essay & research plan

Pages that decide while you look

For twenty years, a web page has changed its mind only between loads. Fast judgment models make it possible for a page to recompose itself while you are still reading. Here is what I think that changes, and how I want to study it.

Yeh Jun Lee · September 2026 · 9 min read

The unit of adaptation is the page load

Personalization on the web is older than most of the people doing it. Yet almost all of it happens at one moment: when the page is requested. The server looks at what it knows, assembles a page, and sends it. After that the page is fixed. If the site wants to show you something different, you have to give it a reason to rebuild — a refresh, a click, a "load more," a new search.

That is not a design philosophy. It is a cost constraint. Deciding what to show takes a judgment, and good judgments have been expensive and slow. A large language model can make a nuanced call about what a visitor is looking for, but not a hundred times per visit, for every visitor, cheaply enough to put in front of an ordinary product page.

What changed

In September 2026 a company called TypeSafe AI released Jev, which it describes as a "System One" decision model. The distinction is useful. A language model writes prose that a person reads. A decision model takes messy input and returns a typed judgment that software consumes: one answer from a pre-approved set of options, plus a probability for its choice.

The numbers the company reports are what make this interesting for marketing. Jev runs on a comparatively small backbone, costs on the order of four cents per thousand judgments, and lands within about three percentage points of a frontier-model judge on preference and factuality tasks — at well under one percent of the cost. Used in a cascade, where confident calls are accepted and uncertain ones escalated to a larger model, it reportedly retains nearly all of the larger model's accuracy.

Whether those exact figures hold up independently remains to be seen. But the direction is clear, and it removes the constraint. When a judgment costs a fraction of a cent, a page can afford to ask "what should this visitor see next?" continuously rather than once.

The page load was never the right moment to decide. It was just the only moment we could afford.

What a deciding page looks like

The shape I have in mind is modest and, I think, practical:

  1. The page is built from a fixed set of modules — a specification table, a lifestyle image, a comparison with a cheaper model, a review summary, a size guide, a sustainability note. These are the pre-approved options. Nothing is generated on the fly.
  2. The visitor's behavior is the input — where they pause, what they scroll past, what they hover over and abandon, what they searched for to get here.
  3. A fast judgment picks the next move: which module to surface, what to emphasize, what to fold away. The decision is a label with a probability, and it can be logged.
  4. When the model is unsure, it doesn't act. Low-confidence judgments either leave the page alone or escalate to a slower, more careful call.

The effect should feel less like a page rearranging itself and more like a good salesperson who notices you keep glancing at the price tag and quietly mentions the payment plan — without making you ask.

Why this is a marketing question, not just an engineering one

Engineering will make this possible. Whether it is a good idea depends on people, and that is where I think the interesting research is.

A page that changes while you look can feel helpful or uncanny. People tolerate being shown a personalized page. They may react very differently to watching one adapt in real time. There is likely a threshold where responsiveness turns into a sense of being watched — and where it lies is an empirical question.

Consistency is part of trust. Two colleagues comparing a product will see different pages. For some categories that is harmless. For anything involving price or eligibility it is not, and the modules a model is allowed to choose among need to be designed with that in mind.

Someone has to check whether the judgments are right. A decision model's great virtue is that it commits: it returns a choice and a probability. That makes it testable in a way a vague personalization engine is not. If the model says "this visitor wants a cheaper alternative, 0.8," we can check how often visitors at 0.8 actually do. This is the part closest to my own work, which is about proving that a model-derived measure tracks what people actually think.

Research plan

I want to study this in three steps, from controlled to real.

Study 1 — Does in-view adaptation help, and at what cost?

An online experiment with a realistic product page in three versions: static; personalized once at load; and adapting while the participant reads. Crossed with whether the adaptation is disclosed ("This page adjusts to what you look at"). Outcomes: choice, time to decision, satisfaction with the choice, perceived autonomy, and trust in the retailer. The key question is whether in-view adaptation improves decisions without eroding the sense that the choice was the shopper's own — and whether disclosure fixes or worsens that trade-off.

Study 2 — Are the judgments right?

A validation study. As participants browse, the model makes its calls about what each person wants next. Afterward, participants report what they were actually looking for, and we observe what they choose. The analysis asks two things: how often the model's judgments match people's own accounts and their behavior, and whether its probabilities are calibrated — whether its 0.8s are right about eighty percent of the time. A model that is accurate but overconfident would be exactly the wrong thing to put in charge of a page.

Study 3 — Does it work in the wild?

A field test on live sites I build and run, including the SimSang Brand Fit Index. Visitors are randomly assigned to adaptive or static versions, and the comparison is on engagement and task completion under real conditions. Owning the testbed matters: it makes it possible to run the experiment cleanly and share what was learned.

The bigger bet

Every generation of marketing technology has been shaped by what judgment costs. When judgment was expensive, we segmented: decide once for a whole group. When it got cheaper, we personalized: decide once per person. If it becomes nearly free, the natural unit is neither the group nor the person but the moment — what this person needs right now, while they are still deciding.

That shift will happen whether or not researchers study it. I would like there to be evidence about when it helps people and when it quietly takes something from them, before the default gets set.
