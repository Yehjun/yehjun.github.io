Do discounts favor products that do more?

A price cut applies to everything on sale. So why might it help a storage bin that fits every room more than one made only for the kitchen? Early evidence from thousands of Amazon listings.

Yeh Jun Lee · September 2026 · 10 min read

Picture two drawer organizers, both twenty percent off. One is sold as a kitchen organizer. The other says it works in the kitchen, the bathroom, the bedroom, and the garage. The discount is identical. Economically, nothing about the sale makes the flexible one a better deal relative to the specific one.

And yet I suspect the sale helps the flexible one more.

The hunch

Think about the last time you bought something because it was on sale. Did you already know exactly what you would use it for? Often, I suspect, the honest answer is "roughly." The sale came first; the specific use came later.

That is the thought behind this project. My conjecture is that promotions do something beyond lowering a price. They create a reason to buy now, which may often be before a shopper knows exactly what they will use the thing for. People stock up, buy ahead, pick something up because the price is right.

If you are buying before the use is settled, a product that covers more possible uses is worth more. Flexibility is an option, and options are most valuable under uncertainty. So the chain I have in mind is:

There is a close precedent. Aydinli, Bertini, and Lambrecht (2014, Journal of Marketing) showed that a promotion applied across a whole choice set can still change which option people prefer — it makes purchases feel less consequential, so people deliberate less and lean on feeling. So "an equal discount shifts relative preference" is not new. What would be new is the specific direction: toward products that keep more uses open.

Looking at the market

Before running experiments, I wanted to know whether any trace of this shows up in real marketplace data. Amazon listings make that possible, crudely. A listing shows whether it is on sale, and many show a badge like "2K+ bought in past month" — a coarse, rounded demand signal, but a real one.

I used two kinds of data: public Amazon product datasets (storage products from 2023, electronics accessories from 2025), and a smaller set of listings I collected and audited by hand in September 2026 (laptop docks and travel plug adapters).

The harder part is measuring breadth. I coded it from what the listing itself claims:

  • Storage products: how many different rooms the product names — kitchen, bathroom, bedroom, garage, laundry, living room, nursery.
  • Electronics accessories and docks: how many platforms it says it works with — Windows, Mac, ChromeOS, iOS, Android, Linux.
  • Travel adapters: whether it is universal/worldwide or made for one region, such as Europe or the UK.

Then the question is simple: is the demand bump from being on sale larger for broader products? Statistically, that is an interaction between on sale and breadth, holding constant price, reviews, rating, and title length. Title length matters: a longer title simply has more space to list rooms or platforms.

What came out

Start with the raw picture, before any statistics. Split the large sample into thirds by how broad each product is relative to similar products, and compare typical sales for listings on sale and not.

That is only suggestive: broader products might differ in price, popularity, or category. So the next step holds those things constant.

The clearest evidence comes from the largest sample.

SampleResult
Storage products and electronics accessories from public datasets, about 3,600 productsDiscounted products with broader coverage showed a larger demand bump. With breadth measured relative to similar products, and price, reviews, rating, title length, and product family held constant, the interaction was positive and reliable (b ≈ .26, t ≈ 3.2). It held in each domain separately, and the combined estimate across the two was b ≈ .24 (t ≈ 3.6).
Laptop docks and travel plug adapters, hand-collected (about 250 listings; a few dozen per category after strict cleaning)No reliable interaction. Estimates were imprecise and mixed in direction. These samples have far less statistical power than the large one.
Same large sample, breadth split into yes/no (one use vs. several)Weaker and not statistically reliable. A two-way split discards much of the information about how many uses a product covers.

To give a sense of size: the outcome is the log of monthly purchases, so an interaction of about .26 means that for a product one standard deviation broader than similar products, being on sale went with a roughly 30% larger sales bump. That is approximate, given how coarse the purchase badges are.

What makes me take this seriously is where it showed up. Storage bins and laptop accessories have almost nothing in common — different shoppers, different prices, different reasons to buy. The only thing they share is that some products do more than others. And in both, the sale seemed to favor the ones that do more.

What this can and can't show

These are cross-sectional market data, so they establish a pattern rather than a cause. The sales badge covers the past month while the discount reflects the listing when it was collected; "regular price" means no visible discount label, which does not rule out coupons; and broad and narrow products may differ in ways the listing doesn't reveal, although price, reviews, rating, and product family are held constant. The next steps are designed to address these directly.

What comes next

A panel, not a snapshot. The right market test tracks the same products over time as they go on and off sale, so each product serves as its own comparison. The question becomes: when this product goes on sale, does its demand rise more if it covers more uses?

A shopping experiment. Participants fill a limited basket from a set of products, some broad and some specific, with a session-wide promotion either on or off. If the account is right, the promoted sessions should produce baskets with a larger share of flexible products — and the shift should be strongest when people haven't been told what they're shopping for.

These are working notes on an early-stage project. The market analyses are exploratory and cross-sectional, and the results have not been peer reviewed. If you have panel data on promotions, or work on pricing and product design, I'd be glad to collaborate: yehjun@vt.edu.
