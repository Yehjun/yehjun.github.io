Do discounts favor products that do more?

A price cut applies to everything on sale. So why might it help a storage bin that fits every room more than one made only for the kitchen? Early evidence from thousands of Amazon listings.

Yeh Jun Lee · September 2026 · 8 min read

Picture two drawer organizers, both twenty percent off. One is sold as a kitchen organizer. The other says it works in the kitchen, the bathroom, the bedroom, and the garage. The discount is identical. Economically, nothing about the sale makes the flexible one a better deal relative to the specific one.

And yet I suspect the sale helps the flexible one more.

The hunch

Promotions do something beyond lowering a price. They create a reason to buy now — often before a shopper knows exactly what they will use the thing for. Nobody discovers a specific shelf problem the moment a sale starts. People stock up, buy ahead, pick something up because the price is right.

If you are buying before the use is settled, a product that covers more possible uses is worth more. Flexibility is an option, and options are most valuable under uncertainty. So the chain I have in mind is:

promotion → buy now → purchase before the use is decided → breadth is worth more

There is a close precedent. Aydinli, Bertini, and Lambrecht (2014, Journal of Marketing) showed that a promotion applied across a whole choice set can still change which option people prefer — it makes purchases feel less consequential, so people deliberate less and lean on feeling. So "an equal discount shifts relative preference" is not new. What would be new is the specific direction: toward products that keep more uses open.

Looking at the market

Before running experiments, I wanted to know whether any trace of this shows up in real marketplace data. Amazon listings make that possible, crudely. Each product page shows whether it is on sale, and many show a badge like "2K+ bought in past month" — a coarse, rounded demand signal, but a real one.

The harder part is measuring breadth. I coded it from what the listing itself claims:

  • Storage products: how many different rooms the product names — kitchen, bathroom, bedroom, garage, laundry, living room, nursery.
  • Electronics accessories: how many platforms it says it works with — Windows, Mac, ChromeOS, iOS, Android, Linux.

Then the question is simple: is the demand bump from being on sale larger for broader products? Statistically, that is an interaction between on sale and breadth, with price, reviews, and rating held constant.

What came out

The honest summary is: a signal in the large sample, nothing in the small ones.

SampleResult
Laptop docks and travel plug adapters, collected and hand-audited (a few hundred listings)No reliable interaction. Some cells looked suggestive; the regressions did not hold up.
Storage products and electronics accessories combined, about 3,600 productsDiscounted products with broader coverage showed a larger demand bump. Measuring breadth relative to similar products, the interaction was positive and clearly distinguishable from zero (b ≈ .26, t ≈ 3.2), and it held in both domains separately.
Same sample, breadth as a simple yes/no (one use vs. several)Much weaker, not statistically reliable.

So there is something here, but it is fragile to how breadth is measured and it disappears in small, carefully cleaned categories. That is exactly the kind of pattern that deserves caution.

Why I don't trust it yet

A snapshot of a marketplace cannot tell you what caused what. Three problems in particular:

  • Timing. The sales badge covers the past month. The discount is whatever is showing today. The two windows don't line up.
  • Hidden promotions. "Not on sale" only means no visible percentage-off label. Coupons and member prices can still be there.
  • Different products. Broad and narrow products may differ in quality, features, or brand in ways the listing doesn't reveal. Holding price and reviews constant helps; it doesn't solve it.

What the data can do is tell me the idea is worth testing properly. It cannot tell me the idea is true.

What comes next

A panel, not a snapshot. The right market test tracks the same products over time as they go on and off sale, so each product serves as its own comparison. The question becomes: when this product goes on sale, does its demand rise more if it covers more uses?

A shopping experiment. Participants fill a limited basket from a set of products, some broad and some specific, with a session-wide promotion either on or off. If the account is right, the promoted sessions should produce baskets with a larger share of flexible products — and the shift should be strongest when people haven't been told what they're shopping for.

The mechanism. The key untested link is the first one: does an ordinary sale actually move people to buy before their use is decided? If it doesn't, the rest of the story falls apart, however good the market correlations look.

These are working notes on an early-stage project. The market analyses are exploratory and cross-sectional, and the results have not been peer reviewed.

Reference: Aydinli, A., Bertini, M., & Lambrecht, A. (2014). Price promotion for emotional impact. Journal of Marketing, 78(4), 80–96.
