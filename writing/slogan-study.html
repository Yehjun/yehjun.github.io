Same promise, different feel

Two AI-written slogans can make the same promise and still feel different: one bright and warm, the other dry and cool. In a preregistered experiment, AI models wrote slogans live for each participant, and people preferred the lines whose feel fit the brand.

Yeh Jun Lee · October 2026 · 6 min read

A layer the usual metrics miss

Anyone who uses language models has noticed it: two answers can say the same thing and still feel different. I call this layer sensory coloring, how bright or dark, warm or cool, round or angular a line feels, apart from what it says and how positive it sounds. It builds on embodied cognition and crossmodal correspondence: we learn words through our senses, so meanings carry traces of perception.

Coloring is too subtle to rate by hand at scale, so I measure it with a language-model instrument. On 80 concepts chosen to span each sensory axis, its readings track averaged human ratings at r = .80. The open question was whether the much subtler differences between lines that make the same promise matter to people at all.

The experiment

  1. Each participant saw three fictitious brands, each with a short brief, such as Nookettle, "a neighbourhood cafe that feels like a friend's kitchen."
  2. For each brand, three frontier models (Claude, GPT, and Gemini) wrote 48 slogans on the spot, all making the same brand promise.
  3. While the participant read the instructions, my pipeline read every line on 45 sensory items, in about half a minute per brand, and scored how closely its feel matched the brief's.
  4. Eight lines, spread from the least to the most fitting, were shown in random order, and the participant rated each one.

Many consumer studies test a handful of hand-written stimuli, so a result can reflect those stimuli rather than the theory. Here every participant saw lines written for them moments earlier.

What happened

Preregistered test · 153 participants · 3,672 ratings

Lines whose feel fit the brand were rated more favorably: +.19 points of brand attitude per standard deviation of fit (95% CI .12 to .26, p < .001), controlling for how closely each line matched the brand's message, its sentiment, and word-level features such as length and word frequency.

Same message, different feel

The cleanest comparison holds the message fixed. Within the same person's list, I took pairs of positively worded lines that matched the brand's message almost equally but differed clearly in feel. The line that felt like the brand won 6 in 10 decided pairs (343 pairs from 132 people; +.30 points on average, 95% CI .04 to .57; exploratory).

In each pair, both lines match the brand's message about equally. What separates them is how they feel.

What it means

When a brand lets AI write its copy, it is already choosing a coloring, usually without measuring it. The result points to a simple guard: generate many candidates, set aside the lines that feel least like the brand, and then pick for message. People respond to the coloring of a single line.
