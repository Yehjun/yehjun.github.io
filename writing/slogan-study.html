The sensory side of AI-written slogans

Words carry sensory associations as well as meaning. In a preregistered experiment, slogans that AI wrote on the spot were rated higher when their sensory profile matched the brand's, with message and sentiment held constant.

Yeh Jun Lee · October 2026 · 7 min read

Meaning has a sensory side

Psychologists have long measured more than what words denote. Osgood's semantic differential showed that words carry connotations people agree on, such as how good, strong, or active something seems. Research on crossmodal correspondence shows that people match features across the senses in shared ways: across many languages, a spiky shape is called kiki and a rounded one bouba, and higher-pitched sounds go with brighter colors. In marketing, the sounds of a brand name shape what people infer about the product.

My job market paper builds on this work. Beyond brand names, ordinary words and whole phrases evoke shared sensory associations, such as how dark or bright, warm or cool, or round or angular something seems, and these associations can be measured. Users of language models notice a version of this: two answers can say the same thing, yet one reads warm and the other dry.

Measuring it

These associations are hard to collect from people at scale, because a stable reading of even one word takes many raters. I therefore prompt a language model to rate a text on 45 sensory scales (dark, bright, warm, cool, round, angular, and so on); the 45 ratings form the text's sensory profile. I validate these scores against human ratings and against outcomes the model never sees:

  • Human ratingsOn 80 concepts chosen to span the scales, the instrument's profiles track averaged human ratings at r = .80.
  • Blind imagesImages generated only from a concept's profile, without its name, were matched by people to the right concept above chance in 26 of 28 comparisons.

The experiment

The question was whether people respond to these associations in a single line of copy, where the differences are much subtler than between kiki and bouba.

  1. Each participant saw three of 20 fictitious brands, each described in a short brief (for example, a neighbourhood cafe "that feels like a friend's kitchen").
  2. For each brand, three frontier models (Claude, GPT, and Gemini) wrote 48 slogans on the spot, all making the same brand promise.
  3. Within about half a minute, the pipeline scored every slogan and the brief on the 45 scales. A slogan's sensory fit is the cosine similarity between its sensory profile and the brief's.
  4. Eight slogans, spread from the lowest to the highest fit, were shown in random order, and the participant rated each one.

Generating fresh slogans for every participant reduces the risk that the result reflects a few hand-picked stimuli.

What happened

Preregistered test · 153 participants · 3,672 ratings

Slogans with higher sensory fit were rated more favorably: +.19 points on a 7-point brand-attitude scale per standard deviation of fit (95% CI .12 to .26, p < .001). The model controlled for how closely each slogan matched the brand's message, its sentiment, and word-level features such as length and word frequency. Judged fit with the brand rose in the same way.

Why single examples don't settle it

Single ratings are noisy, and any one pair of slogans can look like a matter of taste. The preference appears consistently once thousands of ratings are pooled. That is why the measure needs to be automated: it can score every candidate in seconds, which no panel of raters can do for copy that AI writes on demand.

What it means for brands using AI

AI-written copy evokes sensory associations whether or not anyone checks them. The results suggest a screening step before audience testing: generate many candidates, set aside those lowest in sensory fit, and then choose on message. The final choice should still be validated with the actual audience.
