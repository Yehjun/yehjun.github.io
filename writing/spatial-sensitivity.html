Research note

Why the same change feels bigger to some people

Put a wall behind an object, and the same distance starts to feel farther. That small fact is the doorway into a larger idea about how people measure change.

Yeh Jun Lee · September 2026 · 7 min read

A ten-dollar difference means different things in different places. Between two sandwiches it is enormous. Between two laptops it barely registers. Consumer researchers have known this for a long time: people don't judge a price against an absolute ruler, they judge it against the range of prices they expect. The same ten dollars occupies a large slice of a narrow range and a sliver of a wide one.

My question is whether the same thing happens with space — and whether people differ in the range they bring to it.

A ruler that isn't fixed

Imagine two people watching an object move one meter. Both agree it moved one meter. For one of them the move feels trivial; for the other it feels like a lot. They are not disagreeing about the world. They are dividing the same movement by a different denominator.

experienced change = physical change ÷ effective reference range

Hold the movement constant and shrink the range, and the movement becomes a bigger fraction of it. I call the resulting signal spatial sensitivity: how large a fixed spatial change feels, which, on this account, is a readout of how compact the reference range someone is currently using happens to be.

The word effective matters. I am not claiming there is a literal box in the head with walls at fixed distances. The operative range is assembled on the fly — from what you can see, what you are paying attention to, what you remember, and what you are trying to do. Some of it may be a stable feature of the person. Some of it clearly is not.

The wall

Which brings me to the manipulation I find most interesting, because it runs against intuition.

If you place a boundary behind a scene — a wall, a frame, anything that closes the space off — you shrink the range the eye has to work with. The same distance now takes up more of the available room. So on this account the scene should make distances feel larger.

A bounded space doesn't pull things closer. It makes the same distance take up more of the room — so it feels farther.

That is the opposite of what a simple proximity story predicts. Enclosed, close-in scenes are usually assumed to make things feel near and immediate. If range compression is what is really going on, the enclosed scene should instead push judgments outward.

In one study, people looked at either a bounded or an open scene and then judged how far away a future solar eclipse felt, and when they would buy glasses to view it. Half of them were asked to actively judge distances in the scene; the other half simply described it. Among people who actively judged the space, the bounded scene made the eclipse feel farther away and pushed their planned purchase later. Among people who only described the scene, the effect did not appear.

Both halves of that result matter. The direction supports the range account over the proximity account. And the boundary condition — the scene only mattered when people used it as a ruler — says something about process: surrounding geometry shapes judgment when it gets recruited into the comparison, not merely because it is there.

What else lines up

The eclipse study is one of five projects in this line of work. None of them is decisive alone. Together they sketch a consistent picture:

  • The manipulation moves the measure. A compact visual induction raised the spatial-sensitivity index sharply relative to an expansive one. So did cognitive load: people holding a long number in memory reported that a fixed movement felt bigger, which fits the idea that fewer accessible comparison points make the effective range narrower.
  • It travels into time. People whose index was higher tended to feel that a delayed reward was further off — the same scaling logic showing up in a different kind of magnitude.
  • It connects to sensory reactivity. In two samples, people with a narrower, more local processing style reported being more easily overwhelmed by sensory input. With the newer index, the same association showed up again. A compact range would make stimulation feel more consequential — not sharper, but bigger.

Why a marketer should care

Almost every consumer environment is a spatial frame. A store aisle, a phone screen, a product shelf, the white space around a package. If the frame changes the range against which distances and changes are read, it can change how long a wait feels, how big a discount looks, or how urgent a deadline seems — and it can do so differently for different people.

That second part is what interests me most. Marketing is used to treating context as something that shifts everyone a little in the same direction. A range parameter implies heterogeneity: the same shelf may compress the world for one shopper and leave another untouched.

What I don't know yet

This is a framework with early evidence, not a finished theory. The honest open questions are the ones that would decide whether it survives:

  • Is spatial sensitivity a stable trait, or mostly a state that resets with context? My data show consistency within a session, not across weeks.
  • Could some of the correlations reflect people who simply use rating scales expansively? That needs objective magnitude tasks and response-style controls to rule out.
  • Do the manipulations change the underlying range, or only the index? If they move only the index, the natural variation between people may still be real while the inductions are not the causal lever I hope they are.

Those are the next studies. If the parameter holds up, it gives consumer research a way to ask not just what changed but how big the change felt, to whom, and why.

This note describes work in progress from my dissertation research. Findings are preliminary and have not yet been peer reviewed.
